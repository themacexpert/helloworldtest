# Burgers

Notes on building a good burger, from the cow to the plate.

## The beef

Grind matters more than grade. A single lean cut makes a dry patty, so most
good burger blends mix a lean muscle with something fattier:

- **Chuck** — the workhorse. Beefy, around 80/20 lean-to-fat, good on its own.
- **Brisket** — adds fat and a deeper, almost smoky flavor.
- **Short rib** — rich and expensive; use it as a third of the blend at most.
- **Sirloin** — lean and mild. Needs a fattier partner to stay juicy.

Aim for roughly 20% fat overall. Below 15% the patty turns dry and crumbly no
matter how carefully you cook it.

## The patty

Handle the meat as little as possible. Overworking it compacts the grind and
gives you a texture closer to meatloaf than a burger. Form loose patties,
salt the outside only, and salt just before it hits the heat — salting early
draws out moisture and starts curing the surface.

Press a shallow dimple into the center. Patties swell as they cook, and the
dimple keeps the finished burger flat instead of domed.

## Cooking

A hot, dry surface beats a grill for most home cooks. Cast iron holds heat
well and gives you edge-to-edge crust from the Maillard reaction, which is
where nearly all of the flavor comes from.

| Doneness    | Pull temp | Notes                                  |
| ----------- | --------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Rare        | 120°F     | Only for whole muscle, not ground beef |
| Medium rare | 130°F     | Best texture if you grind your own     |
| Medium      | 140°F     | The safe crowd-pleaser                 |
| Well done   | 160°F     | USDA-recommended for ground beef       |

Ground beef mixes surface bacteria throughout the patty, so the safety math
differs from a steak. 160°F is the official guidance.

## The bun

The bun is structure, not filler. It has to absorb juice for several minutes
without falling apart. Potato rolls and brioche both work; brioche is sweeter
and browns faster. Toast the cut sides in butter — the toasted layer acts as a
moisture barrier and buys you a few more minutes before things get soggy.

## Assembly

Order changes how the burger eats. A reasonable default, bottom to top:

1. Bottom bun
2. Sauce
3. Lettuce and tomato
4. Patty and cheese
5. Pickles and onion
6. Top bun

Putting greens under the patty keeps the bottom bun from taking the full brunt
of the rendered fat. Pickles go on top so the acid hits your palate early.

## A note on cheese

Add it during the last minute of cooking and cover the pan. The trapped steam
melts the cheese without overcooking the patty underneath. American melts best
because of its emulsifying salts, which keep it from breaking into oil and
curds the way aged cheddar does.
